## From Shiva to Schrödinger: Unravelling Cosmic Secrets with Trika Shaivism & Quantum Insights — by Dr. Mrittunjoy Guha Majumdar
### A Cambridge-trained quantum physicist argues that a 9th–11th-century Kashmiri philosophy of consciousness and 20th-century quantum physics describe the same reality from opposite ends.

*Hay House India, October 2024 · ~238 pages · Goodreads ~4.0/5*

> **Source note:** This summary is built from the publisher's description, the book's published table of contents and several independent reviews. It was not written from a full reading of the text, so chapter-level detail is lighter than usual and specific claims are attributed with care.

---

### ELEVATOR SUMMARY

In the 1920s, physicists found that a particle can exist as a spread of possibilities until it is measured, and that two particles can stay linked across any distance. A thousand years earlier, Kashmiri sages such as Abhinavagupta taught that reality is a single, vibrating consciousness that only *appears* split into an observer and things observed. Guha Majumdar is an assistant professor at UPES Dehradun with a doctorate from Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory. He sets the two side by side and argues that the resemblance is more than coincidence: both point to a participatory, interconnected and fundamentally non-dual cosmos.

---

### CORE ARGUMENT

The book's thesis is that **Trika (Kashmir) Shaivism and quantum mechanics converge on three ideas**: the separation between observer and observed is not fundamental; everything is deeply interconnected; and reality is dynamic (vibration and potential) rather than a set of static objects. The author claims Trika intuited through contemplative insight what physics later reached through experiment. Entanglement, superposition and the observer effect become the scientific counterparts of non-duality, the interconnectedness of all existence and the "illusion of separation."

The argument is built in three movements. **Section I** sets up the philosophy. It contrasts dual and non-dual worldviews, places Trika among other Indian schools (notably Advaita Vedanta) and introduces Trika's metaphysics and its doctrine of *Pratyabhijñā*, or Recognition. **Section II** is a physics primer covering quantisation, wave-particle duality, superposition, measurement and entanglement. **Section III** sets the two side by side, concept by concept. The book ends by moving from theory to practice, covering Trika's contemplative methods as a way to *experience* what the physics describes.

---

### KEY IDEAS

1. **Non-duality as the shared foundation.** Trika rejects a hard split between subject and object. The individual self and the universal consciousness (Śiva) are one. The author contrasts this with Advaita Vedanta. Both are non-dual, but Advaita tends to treat the world as illusion (*māyā*), while Trika treats it as the *real self-expression* of consciousness. That world-affirming stance is what lets Trika map onto physics, which studies a world that clearly exists.

2. **Prakāśa and Vimarśa: light and self-reflection.** Trika describes ultimate reality (*Anuttara*, "that beyond which there is nothing") as having two aspects. *Prakāśa* is pure, luminous awareness. *Vimarśa* is that awareness turning back on itself and becoming active, creative energy (Śakti). The book uses this pairing of static ground and dynamic energy, Śiva and Śakti, as the template for its comparison with quantum fields and wave functions.

3. **Pratyabhijñā: liberation as recognition.** The Recognition school teaches that freedom is not something you gain. It is the moment you *recognise* that you were Śiva all along, like a woman who meets her famous lover and only then realises he is the man she has seen many times before. The author treats this as the experiential heart of Trika and the reason the book has a practical side.

4. **Spanda and the quantum vacuum.** *Spanda*, the primordial "throb" or vibration of consciousness, is compared to the restless, vibrating nature of quantum reality and to entanglement. On this view the universe is fundamentally a vibration, not a collection of objects.

5. **The observer participates in reality.** In quantum mechanics, measurement turns a superposition of possibilities into a definite outcome (wave-function collapse). Trika holds that consciousness shapes and manifests the world. The author links the two: in both systems, awareness or observation is not a passive window on reality but part of how reality takes shape.

6. **Entanglement and interconnection.** Particles that interact stay correlated however far apart they move, and on some cosmological readings all matter that came out of the Big Bang is linked in this way. The author presents this as the physical counterpart of Trika's claim that all things are parts of one consciousness and that separation is apparent, not real.

7. **Further mappings.** Section III's comparisons include:

   | Trika Shaivism | Quantum mechanics |
   |---|---|
   | Non-dual consciousness | Entanglement / non-separability |
   | Śiva's cosmic dance (*tāṇḍava*), the play of form and formlessness | Wave-particle duality |
   | Non-absolutism (no single fixed viewpoint) | Heisenberg's uncertainty principle |
   | Consciousness manifests the world | Observation collapses the wave function |
   | Spanda (primordial vibration) | Vibrating quantum fields / vacuum |

### NOTABLE EXAMPLES / ILLUSTRATIONS

- **The Pañca Kośas (five sheaths).** The book explains the layered self: physical, energetic, mental-emotional, wisdom and bliss bodies. The true Self lies beyond all five layers, a picture of identity that peels away like an onion until only awareness is left.
- **Classical vs quantum energy.** Classical physics treats energy as a smooth, continuous ramp. Quantum physics treats it as a staircase of discrete packets. The author uses this break with "common sense" to make the reader more open to Trika's equally counter-intuitive claims.
- **Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra's 112 techniques.** Trika's catalogue of awareness meditations, such as attending to the gap between two breaths, is presented as a contemplative "laboratory" for directly testing non-dual insight.

### CRITICAL ANALYSIS

**The book's core bet:** that structural *resemblance* between a contemplative metaphysics and a physical theory is evidence of a shared truth, not just a rich metaphor.

**Strengths**
- **Rare dual credentials.** The author is a working quantum physicist (Cavendish PhD), not a spiritual writer borrowing physics vocabulary. Reviewers note a real command of both fields and frequent reference to Sanskrit sources.
- **Puts Trika on the map.** Most "Eastern mysticism meets physics" books focus on Taoism, Buddhism or Vedanta. This one introduces general readers to Kashmir Shaivism, which is lesser known but philosophically sophisticated.
- **Separates the sections.** Keeping philosophy, physics and comparison in separate sections lets readers judge each part on its own terms.
- **Includes practice.** It goes beyond theory into meditative techniques.

**Limitations**
- **Parallels over divergences.** The most common criticism is that the book stresses resemblances and skips real differences. In physics, "observer" means any irreversible measurement interaction, not necessarily a conscious mind, and the consciousness-causes-collapse reading is a minority view among physicists. One reader caricatured the logic as: *"A believes in interconnectedness. B shows interconnectedness. Therefore connected."*
- **Interpretation dependence.** Quantum mechanics has several competing interpretations (Copenhagen, Many-Worlds, pilot-wave, decoherence-based, QBism). The parallels fit some much better than others, and a skeptic will want that stated outright.
- **Uneven accessibility.** It is pitched at a general audience, but readers report it is dense, sometimes repetitive, and assumes background in at least one of the two fields.
- **Inherits the genre's problems.** Like *The Tao of Physics*, it risks being read as "ancient sages knew quantum mechanics." The fairer claim is conceptual kinship, not anticipation.

**Best for:** Readers drawn to consciousness studies, Indian philosophy or science-spirituality dialogue, especially those who know some Vedanta or meditation and want a rigorous introduction to Kashmir Shaivism.

**Skip if:** You want a strictly physics-first treatment of quantum foundations, or you are allergic to metaphysical readings of quantum mechanics.

**Connects to:** Fritjof Capra's *The Tao of Physics*; Erwin Schrödinger's own Vedanta-inspired *What Is Life?* and *My View of the World* (the title's namesake was famously drawn to Indian non-dualism); David Bohm's implicate order; debates on the "hard problem" of consciousness.

---

### KEY TAKEAWAYS

- **Hold the analogy loosely but seriously.** Use the parallels as lenses for thinking, not as proofs. Ask in each case: *is this the same claim, or the same shape of claim?*
- **Non-dual does not mean world-denying.** Trika's key move is that the world is consciousness expressing itself, not illusion. That reframes spiritual practice as engaging with life rather than escaping it.
- **Recognition, not acquisition.** The Pratyabhijñā idea that you don't become something new, you recognise what you already are, is a useful frame for contemplative practice.
- **Experiment experientially.** Try one Vijñāna Bhairava technique, such as resting attention in the pause between breaths, and treat it as the author suggests: a first-person experiment in non-dual awareness.
- **Learn which quantum claims are physics and which are interpretation.** The book is a good prompt to find out which quantum "weirdness" is established experimentally (entanglement, superposition) and which is philosophical interpretation (the role of consciousness).

### FURTHER READING

- **Fritjof Capra — *The Tao of Physics*.** The founding text of the physics-mysticism genre, useful for comparing methods.
- **Christopher Wallis (Hareesh) — *Tantra Illuminated*.** The best modern scholarly-practical introduction to Trika Shaivism and Abhinavagupta.
- **Jaideva Singh — *Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam: The Secret of Self-Recognition*.** A translation of Kṣemarāja's core Recognition text.
- **Adam Becker — *What Is Real?*** A clear, critical history of the quantum interpretation debates and a needed counterweight.
