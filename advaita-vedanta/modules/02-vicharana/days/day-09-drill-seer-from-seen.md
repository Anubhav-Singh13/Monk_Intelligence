# Day 9 — DRILL I: Seer from Seen

> **Today's one idea (the drill target):** Discriminate the seer from the seen *live* — on a sound, a sensation, an emotion, a thought, a memory, the sense of "me", the act of attending, and finally the witness itself — until you see that the witness can never be found as an object.
> **Reading time:** ~50 min (drills take longer than reading — that's the point) · **Prereqs:** [Day 5](../../01-shubheccha/days/day-05-who-is-asking.md), [Day 6](./day-06-seer-and-seen.md), [Day 7](./day-07-the-five-sheaths.md), [Day 8](./day-08-three-states-one-witness.md)
> **Primary source for today:** *Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka*, verse 1 — Swami Nikhilananda (trans.), Sri Ramakrishna Ashrama, 1931.
> **Before you start:** Without looking — *state the anvaya–vyatireka argument from Day 8 in two sentences: what is present in waking, dream and deep sleep, and why does that make it none of the three?*

---

## How to use this drill

This page is a gym, not a library. Each drill asks you to *do* something with your own experience for a few minutes and write a short answer — **before** you open the hint, and before you open the worked solution. Reading the solutions first is like watching someone else lift the weights: you'll understand the movement and gain no strength.

The drills get harder. Drills 1–3 work on things that are obviously "out there" or "in the body"; drills 4–6 move into the mind and the sense of self, where the seer and the seen are easiest to confuse; drills 7–8 press on the exact point where the discrimination either clicks or doesn't. If a drill produces discomfort — "I can't tell", "this feels circular", "I'm going round in loops" — that is not failure. It is the signal that you have reached the bottleneck this course is built around. Stay there a little longer than is comfortable.

For every drill, use the same three tests from Day 6. Call them **the three checks**:

1. **Change:** does it arise, vary or pass?
2. **Number:** is it one of many — distinguishable from other contents?
3. **Objecthood:** can it be noticed, described, pointed at?

If yes to any → *seen*.

---

### Drill 1 — A sound

Close your eyes. Wait until a sound arises — traffic, a fan, a bird, your own breathing. For two minutes, track it: when it starts, how it changes, when it fades. Then answer in writing: (a) apply the three checks to the sound; (b) when the sound stopped, did anything else stop with it? (c) Was the *hearing* of the next sound a different hearing, or the same?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
For (c), compare the sound of a car with the sound of a bird. The sounds differ; does the being-heard differ?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) The sound arises, varies in pitch and loudness, passes (change ✓); it's distinguishable from other sounds (number ✓); you can describe it (objecthood ✓). Seen.<br/>
(b) When the sound stopped, the sound stopped — nothing else. The space in which it had been heard was not interrupted. You were not "switched off" between sounds.<br/>
(c) The car and the bird are different sounds; the hearing-awareness is not two hearings. This is Day 6's <em>one/many</em> property in its simplest form: the <em>Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka</em>'s "the eye, remaining one, sees many forms" (v. 2), applied to the ear.<br/>
<strong>Common slip:</strong> answering (c) by saying "the hearing is in my ears". The ears are an instrument — the eye in verse 1's chain. Ringing in the ears is itself heard. Push one step further back.
</details>

### Drill 2 — A bodily sensation

Find a sensation: the pressure of your body on the chair, warmth in your hands, an itch, a mild ache. For two minutes, examine it closely. Does it have edges? A location? Does it pulse or stay still? Then answer: (a) the three checks; (b) which of Day 7's sheaths does it belong to; (c) is the sensation *in* the body, or is the body (as experienced) made of sensations like this one?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
For (c): close your eyes and try to find "the body" apart from sensations. What do you actually find?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) It shifts, pulses, fades (change ✓); it's here and not there (number ✓); it's describable (objecthood ✓). Seen.<br/>
(b) The <em>annamaya</em> (physical body) as it appears, and the <em>prāṇamaya</em> (vitality) in its pulsing and warmth. The sensation is a sheath-event (<a href="./day-07-the-five-sheaths.md">Day 7</a>).<br/>
(c) With eyes closed, "the body" turns out to be a loose cloud of sensations — pressure here, warmth there, some tingling — plus a mental image of a body-shape stitched over them. Both the sensations and the image are seen. This doesn't prove there is no physical body; it shows that the body <em>as you experience it</em> is on the seen side, exactly as the Taittirīya's first sheath says. The scabbard is made of things the sword can feel.<br/>
<strong>Common slip:</strong> "But <em>I</em> feel the sensation, so I am in the body." That "I" is a location-feeling — a sensation of being behind the eyes or in the chest — and it's on the list of things to check in Drill 6.
</details>

### Drill 3 — An emotion

Recall something mildly irritating from the past few days — not a trauma, just an annoyance — until a little of the feeling actually arises now. Then, for two minutes, watch the feeling rather than the story. Where is it in the body? Is it rising, peaking, fading? Answer: (a) the three checks; (b) at its peak, did the irritation feel like *something you have* or *something you are*? (c) What happened to the irritation when you watched it rather than fed it?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
For (b), notice the grammar: "I am irritated" vs "irritation is here." Which matches what you observed?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) It rises and falls (change ✓); it has a distinct flavour, unlike sadness or fear (number ✓); you can locate and describe it — heat in the chest, tightness in the jaw (objecthood ✓). Seen.<br/>
(b) At its peak most people find it feels like "I am irritated" — the emotion momentarily takes the shape of the self, like the scabbard taking the shape of the sword. When you step back to watch it, the grammar shifts: "irritation is here, being noticed." The first is identification; the second is accurate.<br/>
(c) Usually it weakens or changes texture. That's a side-effect, not the goal — the drill isn't an anger-management technique. The point is that the irritation <em>could</em> be watched, which places it on the seen side. It belongs to the <em>manomaya kośa</em>. The witness was not irritated; it was aware of irritation.<br/>
<strong>Common slip:</strong> "I made the emotion go away, so I'm in control of it." Control belongs to the intellect (<em>vijñānamaya</em>) — also seen. The witness doesn't control; it illumines.
</details>

### Drill 4 — A thought

Sit for three minutes and simply wait for thoughts. Each time one arises, label it silently *"thought"* and let it go. Count them if you like. Then pick one and examine it: (a) where did it come from? (b) where did it go? (c) did you *think* the thought, or did you *notice* it arriving? (d) Was there any gap between thoughts — and if so, was that gap known?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
For (c): did you decide in advance which thought would come next? For (d): recall Day 8 — what was known in deep sleep, when no thoughts were present?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a)–(b) Nobody can find where thoughts come from or go to; they appear and disappear (change ✓, number ✓, objecthood ✓ — seen).<br/>
(c) Almost everyone, looking honestly, finds that thoughts <em>arrive</em> — you notice them rather than author them. The sense of authorship ("I thought that") usually comes a moment <em>afterwards</em>, as another thought. So even "I am the thinker" is a thought, seen.<br/>
(d) Gaps between thoughts are often noticed — brief moments of quiet. And they are <em>known</em>: you can report "there was a gap." This is Day 8's deep-sleep argument in miniature: the absence of thought is known, so the knower is not a thought. <a href="./day-08-three-states-one-witness.md">Day 8</a>'s row "awareness of what is present — present in all three" applies within a single minute.<br/>
<strong>Common slip:</strong> trying to <em>stop</em> thoughts to "find the witness in the gap." The witness is not in the gaps any more than in the thoughts; it is what both are known by. A quiet mind makes the discrimination easier to see; it doesn't produce what is seen.
</details>

### Drill 5 — A memory

Recall a specific scene from childhood — a room, a face, a place. Let it become vivid. Then answer: (a) the three checks on the memory-image; (b) the child in that memory — is that child you? What, exactly, is common to that child and you now? (c) Was the *awareness* in which the child experienced that scene a different awareness from the one in which you now recall it?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
For (b), list what has changed: body, size, beliefs, vocabulary, fears, tastes. What hasn't?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) The memory-image appears, is vivid or faint, fades (change ✓); it's one memory among thousands (number ✓); you can describe it (objecthood ✓). Seen — and seen <em>now</em>, as a present image.<br/>
(b) Almost everything in the list has changed — every cell, most beliefs, the voice, the fears. What's common is not any content but that <em>experience was being known</em> then and is being known now. The sense of "that was me" rests on this, not on any shared feature.<br/>
(c) There is no evidence of two awarenesses. The contents (child's world, adult's world) are as different as the waking and dream worlds of Day 8; the knowing of them shows none of the features of a changing thing. This is <em>anvaya–vyatireka</em> stretched across decades instead of across one night.<br/>
<strong>Common slip:</strong> "The memory proves a continuous person." It proves continuity of <em>something</em>; the drill asks you to see that the something is not the person (which changed completely) but the awareness. Recall Day 8's objections, though: memory is good evidence, not proof — the drill is pointing, not demonstrating.
</details>

### Drill 6 — The sense of "me"

This is the first hard one. Sit quietly and look for the feeling of being *me* — the one who is doing this drill. Don't think about it; look for it the way you looked for the sensation in Drill 2. Where is it? Behind the eyes? In the chest? Is it a feeling, an image, a thought, a mood? For three minutes, locate it as precisely as you can. Then answer: (a) what did you actually find? (b) the three checks on it; (c) which sheath does it belong to?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
It often shows up as a subtle tension behind the eyes or forehead, or a "centre" in the chest, along with the thought "I". Does that tension change when you relax your face?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) Typical findings: a location-feeling (behind the eyes, in the head, in the chest); a subtle contraction or effort; a recurring thought "I"/"me"; a mood of being the one in charge. Sometimes nothing definite at all, which is itself a finding.<br/>
(b) The location-feeling shifts when you relax the face or move attention (change ✓); it is distinguishable from other sensations (number ✓); you just described it (objecthood ✓). Seen. The "I"-thought appears and disappears (seen). In deep sleep it is absent entirely (<a href="./day-08-three-states-one-witness.md">Day 8</a>) — so by <em>anvaya–vyatireka</em> it cannot be the awareness that is present in deep sleep.<br/>
(c) The <em>vijñānamaya</em> — the intellect's sense of being the doer and knower — mixed with the <em>annamaya</em> feeling of a location. This is the most convincing sheath of all because it takes the shape of the seer most exactly. It is the sword's scabbard pretending to be the hand that holds the sword.<br/>
<strong>Important:</strong> this does not mean "there is no me" in a bleak sense. It means that the <em>sense</em> of me — a felt object — is not the seer. You remain; it is the misidentification that has been caught. If this drill felt destabilising, that's normal; Day 12 explains exactly what has been caught here (<em>adhyāsa</em>).
</details>

### Drill 7 — The act of attending

Harder still. Choose any object — a sound, a sensation — and deliberately attend to it. Then switch attention to another object. Switch back. Do this a dozen times. Now turn to the *attending itself*: the moving, focusing, switching. Answer: (a) is attention seer or seen? (b) when attention moved from object A to object B, was the awareness *of* the moving also moving? (c) Recall [Day 4's tenth man](../../01-shubheccha/days/day-04-the-tenth-man.md): what in this drill corresponds to counting?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
Attention has direction, effort, narrowness or width. Can you notice those qualities? Then they are qualities of something seen.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) Seen. Attention has qualities — narrow or wide, effortful or easy, steady or jumpy — and it moves (change ✓). You can notice it switching (objecthood ✓). It belongs to the mind and intellect (<em>manomaya</em>/<em>vijñānamaya</em>). This surprises people because attention <em>feels</em> like the seer — it is the spotlight with which we look. But the spotlight is visible: you knew it moved.<br/>
(b) No. The awareness of attention moving did not move. You knew "now on A, now on B" from a place that wasn't itself on A or B. That's the torch from Day 6: the beam moves; the light's being-on doesn't travel with it.<br/>
(c) Attention is the counting. Each man in the Tenth Man story directed his attention outward and counted nine — every object was correctly counted; the counter was left out. Attending, however refined, is always aimed at something; whatever it is aimed at is the nine. The tenth is never at the end of a beam of attention.<br/>
<strong>Common slip:</strong> "So I should attend to awareness itself." Try it in Drill 8 — and see what happens.
</details>

### Drill 8 — The witness itself

The last drill, and the point of the page. Set a timer for five minutes. Your task: **find the witness.** Not think about it — find it, the way you found the sound, the sensation, the emotion, the thought, the memory, the "me", the attending. Use attention, effort, stillness — whatever you like. Each time something appears that might be "it", apply the three checks honestly. Keep going until the timer ends.

Then answer: (a) list everything that showed up as a candidate; (b) the verdict on each; (c) what does the failure of the search show? (d) Use [Day 4](../../01-shubheccha/days/day-04-the-tenth-man.md)'s passer-by: what sentence would the passer-by say to you now, and why can no amount of further searching replace it?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>
Recall the <em>Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka</em>'s fourth line: <em>sākṣī dṛg eva na tu dṛśyate</em> — "the witness is the seer, and is never seen." What was the search instrument you were using?
</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) Typical candidates: a sense of space or openness; a stillness; a faint glow or light; a feeling of presence; a blank; the thought "this is it"; a sense of "I am"; frustration; the thought "there is nothing here."<br/>
(b) Every one is seen: each arose, had a quality, and could be described. The space and stillness are subtle states of the <em>manomaya</em> or <em>ānandamaya</em> sheaths; the light is an image; the blank is known as a blank; "this is it" and "there is nothing here" are thoughts. All on the seen side — just like Vidyāraṇya's "void" (<a href="./day-07-the-five-sheaths.md">Day 7</a>).<br/>
(c) The failure is <strong>the result</strong>, not a failure of effort. You were searching with attention (Drill 7), which only ever finds objects. The witness is not an object, so the search was structurally guaranteed to return only seen things. This is Day 6's third property — <em>never an object</em> — confirmed by direct test rather than by argument. And notice: throughout the five minutes, every candidate was <em>known</em>. The searching was being witnessed. The witness wasn't missing from the search; it was what the search was happening in.<br/>
(d) The passer-by says: <em>daśamas tvam asi</em> — "you are the tenth." Applied here: <em>you are the witness you were looking for.</em> No further searching helps because searching is counting, and counting always reaches nine. The ignorance is not removed by finding a tenth object but by a <em>means of knowledge</em> (<a href="../../01-shubheccha/days/day-04-the-tenth-man.md">Day 4</a>'s <em>śabda-pramāṇa</em>) that turns the attention round to the one who was counting. That is why the course leans on the teaching (the Upaniṣad's sentences) rather than on searching harder — and why Day 16's <em>tat tvam asi</em> has the same grammar as the passer-by's sentence.<br/>
<strong>The one thing to take away:</strong> <em>If you found it, it isn't it. That you are finding, is.</em>
</details>

**Transfer — apply it:**

> Tomorrow, at one ordinary moment — waiting for a kettle, sitting in a meeting, walking — run a 30-second version of the drill ladder: *sound → sensation → feeling → thought → "me" → attending*, labelling each "seen". Write one sentence afterwards: *the input* (the ordinary moment), *what today's drill does to it* (sorts every element into seen, leaving only what all of it appeared to), and *the output* (what was different about that moment when nothing in it was taken to be you). If nothing comes to mind in 60 seconds, re-read Drill 1 and try again.

## Scoring yourself

Give yourself one point for each drill where your written answer *before* opening the solution reached the same verdict for the same reason. **6–8:** the discrimination is live — keep using the 30-second ladder daily. **3–5:** normal at this stage; repeat Drills 6–8 tomorrow before Day 10. **0–2:** re-read [Day 6](./day-06-seer-and-seen.md)'s rule and do Drills 1–4 again slowly — the later drills rest on them.
