# Day 24 — Drill II: Mechanism vs. Narrative

> **Today: the adversarial gym.** No new concepts. Eight scenarios, each wrapped in a *plausible, popular narrative* that gets the mechanism wrong — and would cost you money. Your job: spot the trap, state the correct mechanism, and give the real market read.
> **Reading time:** ~45 min · **Prereqs:** [Days 14–23](../overview.md)
> **Before you start:** Recall Day 23 in one sentence, no looking — *on RBI policy day, what actually moves markets if not the rate decision?*

**How to do this drill:**
For each scenario, before opening the solution: (1) write the *one-line narrative* the crowd believes, (2) name *why it's wrong* (the mechanism it ignores), and (3) give the *correct read*. The narratives here are all things you'll genuinely hear on TV and social media — that's the point. A right instinct with a wrong mechanism still fails the next variation, so state the mechanism explicitly.

The three master-traps, now weaponised: **(a) the sign flips depending on *why* something moved; (b) "the crowd believes X" is itself information (positioning); (c) the same event means opposite things in different regimes.**

---

### Rep 1 — "Rate cuts are always bullish"

**Narrative:** "The RBI just cut rates 50 bp in an emergency inter-meeting move. Cuts are bullish — buy stocks!"

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** it ignores *why* the RBI cut and what a cut *signals*. An **emergency, larger-than-normal cut** screams "the RBI sees serious trouble." Via the discount-rate lens (Day 17), the lower `r_f` is *swamped* by a spiking **risk premium** (fear, Day 18) and collapsing growth expectations (`g`). **Correct read:** markets often *fall* on emergency cuts (as globally in early 2020) — the cut confirms danger louder than it eases conditions. A cut is only cleanly bullish when it's a *calibrated* easing into a *stable* backdrop, not a panic response. *Always ask: why did they cut?*
</details>

---

### Rep 2 — "Strong GDP is good for stocks"

**Narrative:** "GDP smashed expectations at 8.5%! Great news — the market will rally."

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** "good news is good" is *regime-dependent* (Day 21, Day 22). If the market is in an **inflation-focused / overheating** regime, blockbuster growth means the RBI will have to **hike harder** to cool it → higher `r_f` → higher discount rate. **Correct read:** in an inflation-anxious regime, hot GDP can *sink* stocks ("good news is bad news") because it forces tighter policy; in a growth-starved, disinflationary regime, the same print rallies stocks. **The sign depends on which dial the market fears.** Check: is the market more scared of weak growth or high inflation right now?
</details>

---

### Rep 3 — "The RBI hiked, so the rupee will strengthen"

**Narrative:** "The RBI hiked 25 bp — higher rates mean a stronger rupee, so expect USD/INR to fall."

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** the Day 15/Day 19 sign-flip. What matters is the rate differential *relative to the US*, not India's rate alone. If the **Fed hiked 50 bp the same week**, the India–US gap *narrowed* despite the RBI hiking → capital flows *out* → **rupee weakens**. **Correct read:** "higher domestic rates → stronger currency" holds only *holding the world constant*. When the Fed is hiking faster, the rupee can fall even as the RBI hikes. *Always compare to the US/global path.* This is the single most common currency error.
</details>

---

### Rep 4 — "Inflation fell, buy bonds"

**Narrative:** "CPI dropped from 6.0% to 4.8%! Inflation's beaten — pile into bonds."

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** it reads the *headline* and ignores the *internals* (Day 9, Day 22). If the drop was a **base effect** (last year's month was high) or a one-off **food** decline while **core** inflation is *still rising*, the underlying trend is *not* improving — and the RBI will look through the headline. **Correct read:** check core and the base. If core is sticky/rising, the RBI stays hawkish, bonds *don't* rally (may fall), and "inflation is beaten" is premature. *The headline is the bait; the composition is the signal.*
</details>

---

### Rep 5 — "Gold is an inflation hedge — inflation's up, so buy gold"

**Narrative:** "Inflation is high and rising. Gold is the classic inflation hedge — load up."

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** gold responds most to **real interest rates** (nominal rate minus inflation), not inflation alone. If the RBI (or the Fed) is hiking *aggressively* so that **real rates rise**, gold often *falls* despite high inflation — because gold pays no yield, so higher real rates raise its opportunity cost. **Correct read:** gold shines when inflation is high *and* real rates are low/falling (central bank behind the curve); it struggles when real rates rise even if inflation is high. **Inflation up ≠ gold up — it depends on the real rate.** (The discount-rate lens, Day 17, applied to a zero-cash-flow asset.)
</details>

---

### Rep 6 — "FIIs are selling, the market must crash"

**Narrative:** "Foreigners sold ₹20,000 crore this month — the market is doomed."

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** it looks at one flow in isolation and ignores *who's on the other side* and *positioning* (Day 18, Day 22). India now has a large, sticky **domestic flow** (SIPs, DII buying) that can *absorb* FII selling, and if FIIs are *already* underweight, the marginal seller is exhausted. **Correct read:** FII selling matters, but its *market impact* depends on domestic demand absorbing it and on how *positioned* everyone already is. Heavy FII selling into strong, persistent SIP inflows can be *absorbed* with limited damage — and extreme, capitulatory selling is sometimes a *contrarian* signal (Day 18's overshoot). *A flow is only half the equation; find the other side.*
</details>

---

### Rep 7 — "Huge government infra spending — buy everything"

**Narrative:** "The Budget announced a massive infrastructure and capex push. Bullish for the whole market!"

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** it sees the spending but ignores the *financing* (Day 16). A massive capex push funded by a **wider deficit** means a flood of new G-sec **supply** → **yields rise** (bond prices fall) → the discount rate rises for *every* asset (Day 17), partly offsetting the growth impulse. **Correct read:** it's genuinely bullish for *capex beneficiaries* (infra, capital goods, cement — Day 7), but *not* uniformly bullish: rate-sensitive and richly-valued sectors face a higher discount rate, and bonds sell off. And if the deficit overshoots expectations, the *net* market reaction can be negative. *Spending without checking the financing is half the picture — the two levers can fight (Day 5).*
</details>

---

### Rep 8 — "The yield curve inverted — recession's here, sell now"

**Narrative:** "The G-sec curve just inverted. Inversion means recession — dump equities immediately."

<details>
<summary>Trap & correct read</summary>

**The trap:** two errors. First, inversion is a *lead*, not a *timer* — even in the US it precedes recessions by 6–24 months, and equities frequently *rally* in that window before rolling over (Day 11). Second, **India's curve signal is weak** — the RBI actively manages the curve, the bond market is illiquid and bank-dominated (SLR), so an Indian inversion is a murkier signal than a US one. **Correct read:** treat it as a *directional* warning to *raise vigilance and start rotating toward quality/duration over the coming months*, not a same-day sell signal. And in India, cross-check with growth data and credit spreads (Day 20) rather than trusting the curve alone. *A lead indicator mistimed becomes a bad trade.*
</details>

## Debrief (3 min)

Score: **7–8 traps caught** = you're reasoning from mechanism, not narrative — the course's core goal is met. **4–6** = solid; revisit the reps you missed and the linked day. **Below 4** = the narratives are still overriding the mechanism; re-read Days 17, 19, 21 before the capstone.

The unifying lesson across all eight: **every lazy narrative is a mechanism with a step deleted.** "Cuts are bullish" deletes *why they cut*. "RBI hiked → rupee up" deletes *relative to whom*. "Strong GDP → buy" deletes *which regime*. "Inflation fell → buy bonds" deletes *core vs. base*. "Inflation up → buy gold" deletes *real rates*. "FIIs selling → crash" deletes *the other side*. "Infra push → buy all" deletes *the financing*. "Inversion → sell" deletes *the lag*. Your edge, permanently, is **putting the deleted step back.**

> **Transfer — apply it:** Find one confident macro claim in today's financial media or your feed ("X happened, so buy/sell Y"). Identify the deleted step — the mechanism the narrative skipped. State it as input (the claim) → today's idea (the missing mechanism) → output (the corrected read, which may agree *or* disagree with the claim). Do this daily and you will, within weeks, read markets differently from 90% of the people around you. This is exactly the muscle the capstone (Day 27) tests on live data.
