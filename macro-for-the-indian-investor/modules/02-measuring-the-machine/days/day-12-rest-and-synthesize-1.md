<details>
<summary>Compare to these</summary>

1. **Yield curve (11):** G-sec yields from short to long maturity form a curve whose shape is the bond market's forecast of growth and inflation; inversion warns of expected weakness.
2. **Surprise (3):** Prices already embed expectations, so they move on the gap between actual and expected — the surprise — not on the event itself.
3. **C+I+G+NX (7):** GDP splits by who spends; investment (I) is the volatile ~31% that drives the cycle, consumption (C) the stable ~58% ballast.
4. **Transactions (1):** Every rupee spent is someone's income, so spending ≡ income ≡ output — the economy is one closed loop.
5. **CPI vs WPI (9):** CPI (retail, ~46% food) is the RBI's legal 4%±2% target; WPI (wholesale) reads input costs and margins; core strips food/fuel.
6. **Two levers (5):** The RBI (monetary — repo rate, inflation goal, fast) and the Government (fiscal — spending/taxes, growth goal, slow) steer the short-run economy.
7. **Trend vs cycle (2):** Long-run growth (supply/productivity) is a different thing from the short-run cycle (demand swings); investors trade the cycle.
8. **Repo & G-secs (10):** The repo rate is the anchor price of money; bond prices move inversely to yields; the risk-free G-sec yield underpins every asset's valuation.
9. **Inflation (4):** Sustained rise in the general price level = too much spending chasing too few goods; demand-pull vs. cost-push (food/oil).
10. **Growth pulse (8):** With no monthly payrolls, India reads IIP, PMI, GST, credit, and auto sales to sense the economy between quarterly GDP prints.
11. **Three lenses (6):** GDP counted as output (GVA), income, or expenditure must agree; watch nominal vs. real and the GVA-vs-GDP tax wedge.
</details>

## Step 2 — Re-derive the machine (10 min)

Fill the blanks from memory. This is the whole course-so-far in one chain:

```
A shift in [___A: what people decide to do with money] changes total SPENDING,
which (Day 1) equals total [___B],
which is measured as GDP three ways: output/[___C], income, and expenditure = C + I + G + NX.
Most of the short-run SWING comes from component [___D], because it is the most [___E].
If spending outruns the supply of goods, you get [___F] (Day 4),
which India measures as [___G] and targets at [___H]%.
The RBI responds by moving the [___I] rate,
which re-prices G-secs (price moves [___J] to yield)
and re-shapes the [___K], whose slope forecasts growth.
But markets only move on the [___L] — the gap between actual and expected.
```

<details>
<summary>Filled-in chain</summary>

A = spending/saving/borrowing decisions · B = income · C = GVA · D = investment (I) · E = volatile · F = inflation · G = CPI · H = 4 · I = repo · J = inversely · K = yield curve · L = surprise
</details>

**Questions to answer without looking (probe mechanisms, not definitions):**
1. *Why* must the three ways of measuring GDP give the same number? (Trace it to Day 1.)
2. *Why* does a high-growth stock fall more than a value stock when rates rise unexpectedly?
3. *Why* can India's headline CPI spike while the economy is weak — and what does the RBI look at instead?
4. *Why* does an inverted yield curve signal expected weakness rather than strength?
5. *Why* can the RBI cut the repo rate and the Nifty fall the same afternoon?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Because every transaction has two sides: what's produced is sold (spending) and the proceeds are earned (income). The three totals are one flow counted three ways (Day 1/6).
2. A high-growth stock's value is mostly *distant* future earnings; a higher discount rate shrinks the present value of far-off cash flows the most (long "duration"). Day 10 → Day 17.
3. India's CPI is ~46% food + fuel, so a monsoon or oil shock (cost-push) spikes headline even in a weak economy. The RBI watches **core** (ex-food/fuel) and inflation *expectations* to judge whether to act (Day 9).
4. A long yield ≈ average of expected future short rates. Inversion means the market expects the RBI to *cut* — which it only would if it foresaw weakness. So the shape encodes an expected downturn (Day 11).
5. The cut was expected and already priced; the *surprise* was elsewhere — e.g., hawkish guidance signalling it's the last cut. Markets trade the surprise, not the event (Day 3/10).
</details>

## Step 3 — Apply it (10 min)

**Written reconstruction (do this cold, then compare):** In ~200 words, explain to a hypothetical colleague — a smart equity investor who's never thought about macro — *how a bad monsoon could end up affecting their stock portfolio.* Force yourself to chain at least four of the eleven concepts (e.g., monsoon → food prices → CPI → RBI → rates → discount rate → stocks). Write it, *then* read the model below.

<details>
<summary>Model reconstruction</summary>

A bad monsoon cuts food output. Since food is ~46% of India's CPI (Day 9), retail inflation spikes — a *cost-push* shock (Day 4). The RBI's legal job is CPI at 4% (Day 5, 9). If it fears the food spike is leaking into core inflation and expectations, it holds or hikes the **repo rate** (Day 10). Higher rates lift the risk-free G-sec yield and the whole rate ladder, and can steepen or shift the **yield curve** (Day 11). A higher discount rate lowers the present value of *future* corporate earnings — hurting richly-valued growth stocks most. Meanwhile, food inflation squeezes household budgets, denting *consumption* (C, ~58% of GDP — Day 7), which shows up first in **high-frequency data** like two-wheeler sales (Day 8) before the quarterly GDP confirms it. So a monsoon — a weather event — transmits through inflation, the RBI, and rates into your portfolio. And your stocks will move not when the monsoon fails, but when the CPI print *surprises* versus what the market expected (Day 3).
</details>

If you couldn't chain four concepts, that's your diagnostic — revisit the specific days that broke the chain before moving on.

## Step 4 — Quiz (5 min)

> **Why the questions jump around:** day-ordered questions let you answer by position. These deliberately cross concept boundaries. Answer each cold, before looking.

1. The 10-year G-sec yield falls sharply below the 2-year. In one sentence, what has the bond market just forecast, and about which policy actor? *(Day 11)*
2. A company reports 20% profit growth and the stock *falls*. Give the one-word reason and explain it in a sentence. *(Day 3)*
3. GDP grows 8% but GVA grows only 6%. What is the missing 2 percentage points, and should it make you more or less confident in the "growth"? *(Day 6)*
4. The RBI unexpectedly hikes 50 bp. Explain, chaining two concepts, why your long-duration bond *and* your high-growth stock both fall. *(Days 10 + 2/7)*
5. **Cross-concept:** India's CPI is driven ~46% by food, and the RBI legally targets *headline* CPI. Explain how these two facts *together* create a dilemma the RBI can't fully solve with its only tool — and name the tool. *(Days 4/9 + 5/10)*

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. It forecasts that the RBI will *cut* rates because the economy is heading for weakness/slowdown — an inverted curve pricing expected easing (Day 11).
2. **Surprise.** The market expected *more* than 20% (say 25%), so the surprise was negative even though the news was "good" — price moves on actual-minus-expected (Day 3).
3. The 2 points are **net indirect taxes** (higher GST / lower subsidies) added to GVA to get GDP — not extra real production. Be *less* confident: the underlying activity (GVA) grew slower than the headline; some "growth" is a tax wedge (Day 6).
4. The surprise hike raises the risk-free rate → the bond's price falls inversely to its higher yield (Day 10), *and* the higher discount rate shrinks the present value of the growth stock's distant future earnings (Day 10 → 7's I/valuation logic). Same cause — a higher discount rate — hits both.
5. Because ~46% of CPI is food (supply-driven, cost-push — Day 4/9) but the RBI must hit *headline* CPI with its only tool, the **repo rate**, which works by cooling *demand* (Day 5/10). It cannot grow more food, so to force headline back to 4% after a food shock it must crush demand — risking growth — to offset prices it doesn't control. Targeting headline in a food-heavy economy is the dilemma.
</details>
